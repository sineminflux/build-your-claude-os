# Kill List

These phrases and patterns are banned. If you catch yourself writing any of them, delete and rewrite.

## Hollow Verb Phrases

Verbs that sound active but say nothing about what the thing actually does.

- "built to understand"
- "designed to empower"
- "crafted to deliver"
- "engineered to perform"
- "created to inspire"
- "made to transform"
- "developed to enhance"

The fix: replace with what the thing literally does. "Built to understand your needs" becomes "reads your past orders and suggests sizes."

## Filler Words

Words that pad sentences without adding meaning. Delete them and the sentence gets better.

- seamlessly
- leverage
- elevate
- unlock
- reimagine
- holistic
- robust
- cutting-edge
- innovative
- next-generation
- best-in-class
- world-class
- synergy
- streamline
- optimize (when used vaguely)
- empower
- revolutionize
- disrupt
- "journey" (when used to describe anything that isn't physical travel)
- "space" (as in "the career transition space")
- "landscape" (as in "the AI landscape")
- "navigate" (as in "navigate uncertainty")
- "unpack" (as in "let's unpack this")
- "lean into"
- "double down"
- "deep dive"
- "game-changer"
- "thought leader"

## Buzzword Stacks

Multiple filler words chained together, pretending to be a value proposition.

- "AI-powered solution built to engage"
- "data-driven platform designed to transform"
- "cloud-native infrastructure built for scale"
- "end-to-end solution that drives results"

The fix: name the specific capability, the specific user, and the specific outcome.

## Formulaic Rhythm Tricks

Sentence structures that AI defaults to because they sound punchy but contain no information.

- "No X. No Y. Just Z." (e.g., "No hassle. No complexity. Just results.")
- "X meets Y." (e.g., "Innovation meets simplicity.")
- "Where X meets Y." (e.g., "Where technology meets humanity.")
- "More than just X." (e.g., "More than just a platform.")
- "X, redefined." (e.g., "Customer service, redefined.")
- "The future of X." (e.g., "The future of work.")
- "Let that sink in."
- "Read that again."
- "Full stop."
- "[Noun]. That's it. That's the post."
- "That's the [noun]." as a recap capper (e.g. "That's the loop.", "That's the trick.", "That's the whole game."). A short label that points back at the explanation already given and adds nothing. The fix: delete it, the explanation already landed.

## "Nobody Warns You" Openers

Phrases that frame ordinary observations as secret knowledge. They've become a crutch for internet writing that wants to sound profound without earning it.

- "nobody warns you about"
- "nobody told you"
- "nobody talks about"
- "no one prepares you for"
- "the thing nobody tells you"
- "here's what they don't tell you"
- "Here's the thing about..."
- "The truth about X is..."
- "What I wish someone had told me..."

The fix: just say the thing. If the observation is good, it doesn't need a "secret wisdom" frame to land.

## "It's Not X, It's Y" Pivots

The false-dichotomy sentence structure that sets up a straw man just to knock it down. It sounds like insight but usually just restates something obvious in two parts. This includes the subtler "adjective-flip" variant where you set up one adjective and swap it for another.

- "It's not X. It's Y."
- "This isn't about X. It's about Y."
- "Those aren't X. They're Y."
- "The problem isn't X. The problem is Y."
- "You don't need X. You need Y."
- "Not productive. Generative."
- "Not a career. A practice."
- "Not strategic. Deliberate."
- "Because X is not Y, it is Z."

This pattern has many grammatical disguises. Match the *structure* (negation + pivot to restatement), not just the exact wording. All of these are the same move:

- "That's not X. That's Y." (e.g., "That's not immaturity. That's the cost of never being asked.")
- "[Noun] is not X. It's Y." (e.g., "A career transition is not a straight line. It's a spiral.")
- "[Noun] is not X. [Noun] is Y." (e.g., "Self-permission is not a one-time breakthrough. It's an ongoing practice.")
- "The goal is not a credential. The goal is to ___." (goal-statement variant, negation and pivot split across two sentences)
- "The thing that [verbed] me wasn't X." (setup for "it was Y")
- "[Noun] wasn't X. [Noun] was Y."
- "That's not nothing. That's everything."
- "Not because X, but because Y." (e.g., "Not because I found the answer, but because I stopped waiting for it.")
- "Not because X happened. Because Y happened."
- "Not out of X. Out of Y."
- "I didn't leap. I stepped." (curt verb-swap: negate one verb in a short sentence, then substitute a punchier verb in the next. Same negation-then-pivot, compressed to two beats.)

The "Not because X, but because Y" variant is especially slippery because it hides inside a subordinate clause instead of standing as two separate sentences. It's still the same negation-then-pivot move.

The test: if you could delete the first sentence (the negation) and the second sentence still makes sense on its own, the first sentence was just a runway for a false pivot. Delete it and start with the thing that's actually true.

The fix: if the distinction is real, just state what's true. If it's synonym-swapping dressed as insight, cut it. "Mysteries don't get solved. They get lived with" is stronger than "Those aren't puzzles. They're mysteries." Skip the setup; trust the reader.

These hide especially well inside code: JS strings, prompt/instruction text, alt text, and ARIA labels. Scan those too.

## Anthropomorphizing Abstractions

Giving human qualities to things that don't have them.

- "a crowd that feels every lap"
- "technology that cares"
- "a platform that listens"
- "data that tells a story"
- "AI that understands you"
- "a community that thrives"

The fix: describe what actually happens. "A platform that listens" might mean "users submit feature requests and vote on them." Say that instead.

## Throat-Clearing Openers

Phrases that stall before saying the actual thing. They announce that something important is coming instead of just saying it. Delete them and start where the meaning begins.

- "Somewhere along the way,"
- "Somewhere in [the mess / it / there / all of this]" (also shows up mid-paragraph as a vague transition, not just as an opener; hand-waves past the actual moment)
- "At the end of the day,"
- "When all is said and done,"
- "In today's world,"
- "But here is the uncomfortable truth,"
- "And let's be clear:"
- "Think about it."
- "The question becomes:"
- "But, here is the truth:"
- "Here's what" / "Here is what" as an opener ("Here's what that buys you:", "Here's what happens next", "Here's what I did")

The fix: cut the opener entirely. The sentence that follows it is where the writing actually starts. "Here's what" / "Here is what" is banned even when a concrete list or example follows it: drop the runway and lead straight with the content or the list ("Here's what that buys you:" becomes "That buys you:" or just the list).

## Vague Change Announcements

A line that announces something is changing without naming the change. Content-free: the reader is told a change exists but not what it is.

- "Today I'm changing what this is about."
- "I'm switching things up."
- "Big changes ahead."
- "A new chapter begins."
- "Something is shifting for me."

The fix: name the specific new thing, or open on the concrete moment of the change. Lead with the substance, not the announcement that a change is coming.

## False-Profundity Framing

Phrases that announce insight instead of delivering it. If the observation is good, it doesn't need a hype intro.

- "The truth hits me like a slap"
- "A deeper truth emerged"
- "And then it hit me"
- "Here's what most people miss"
- "This pattern reveals an essential truth:"
- "The truth is, [statement]"
- "Here is the part I keep needing to hear."
- "The thing I have to remind myself is..."
- "What I keep coming back to is..."
- "Here is what I tell myself."

The fix: delete the frame and just state the thing. "The truth is, reality has never been linear" becomes "Reality has never been linear."

## Dramatic Staccato

Adjectives, verbs, or infinitives broken into solo sentences for false dramatic weight. They mimic punchy writing but carry no real force because nothing is grounded.

- "Clean. Efficient. Safe."
- "Unattainable. Unnatural."
- "To pivot. To get back up. To learn and grow."

The fix: attach them to nouns or combine them into real sentences. Better yet, pick the one that matters and say why it matters.

## Candle-Test Phrases

Vague spiritual or self-help language that could be printed on a candle, a yoga studio wall, or a TED talk bumper. It sounds meaningful but specifies nothing.

- "fertile ground of possibility"
- "space to breathe"
- "whisper of possibility"
- "edges of our being"
- "What does this moment invite me to become?"
- "more clarity, more energy, more alignment"
- "journey of self-discovery"
- Any sentence where "possibility" or "potential" does the work a specific noun should be doing

The fix: replace with what actually happened or what specifically changed. "I needed space to breathe" becomes "I needed a month where nobody asked me what I was doing next."

## Abstract Noun Stacking

Multiple abstract nouns piled into one sentence until it becomes white noise. Common in academic-adjacent and consulting-adjacent writing.

- "the simultaneous commitment to purposeful action and graceful acceptance of results beyond our control"
- "This stance creates a sustainable foundation for meaningful work"

The fix: if you can't point to a concrete thing the sentence describes, rewrite it. "Sustainable foundation for meaningful work" is five words that say nothing. What work? What makes it sustainable? Say that instead.

## Dead Cliche Stacking

Two or more cliches placed back to back, where each one is tired on its own but together they become pure filler.

- "You're not climbing a ladder. You're walking a path that unfolds with each step."
- "not because I was failing, but because I was ready to listen to possibility"

The fix: if you've heard both halves before, neither half is doing work. Start over with something specific to the actual situation.

## Sycophantic Openers

AI's reflex to validate before doing anything useful. Skip the compliment. Start with the answer or the work.

- "Great question!"
- "That's a really interesting point."
- "I love this direction."
- "Absolutely!"
- "What a thoughtful approach."

The fix: delete the opener. If the question was good, the quality of your answer will show that.

## Colon Constructions

The "Label: Explanation" format that reads like a slide deck, not like a human talking.

- "The key insight: you don't need permission."
- "The truth: nobody is watching."
- "My take: this matters more than we think."
- "The bottom line: [restates the paragraph above]."

The fix: just say the thing. Drop the label.

## Significance Inflation

Making ordinary observations sound world-historically important. If the observation is good, it doesn't need a drumroll.

- "This changes everything."
- "This is the real paradigm shift."
- "What most people miss is..."
- "The uncomfortable truth is..."
- "Here's what nobody understands about..."

The fix: state it plainly. Let the reader decide if it changes everything.

## Emotional Metaphor Mic-Drops

Ending a paragraph on a feeling-plus-metaphor flourish that sounds profound, adds no information, and breaks the register of the concrete argument around it. It reaches for an emotion the writing hasn't earned and says nothing specific.

- "and then it felt like oxygen"
- "something in me finally exhaled"
- "it felt like coming home"
- "and for the first time, I could breathe"

The fix: end on the concrete thing that actually changed, or cut the line. If the paragraph already made its point, stop there. Don't tack on a mood.

## Strained / Incoherent Metaphors

A clever-sounding image that falls apart when you picture it literally. Distinct from a mismatched collocation (a single bad word-pair): this is a whole metaphor that does not cohere.

- "a LinkedIn robot wearing my name"
- "an algorithm that tucks you in at night"
- "words wearing someone else's face"

The test: picture the image literally. If it cannot hold together (a robot cannot wear a name), cut it and say the plain thing. "drafts that sounded like a LinkedIn robot wearing my name" becomes "drafts that didn't sound anything like me."

## Rule of Three / Parallel Triads

AI defaults to presenting exactly three things in parallel structure. It's a tell.

- "You learn. You build. You grow."
- "Strategic but not rigid. Creative but not chaotic. Personal but not private."
- "One asset at a time. One system at a time. One lever at a time." (the "One X at a time" mantra triad counts, even as an italic sign-off)
- Any sequence of exactly three short parallel phrases

The fix: vary your rhythm. Two things. Four things. One thing explained well. Don't default to triplets.

### Two-beat parallel contrast pairs

The twin of the triad: closing a point with two matched beats, "A does X. B does Y.", usually as a metaphor mic-drop at the end of a paragraph.

- "The chatbot reads you the recipe. The agent's the one at the stove."
- "Automation follows the script. The agent writes its own."
- "A tourist takes photos. A local knows the back streets."

Why it's a tell: AI loves to end by restating the point as a tidy metaphor in two parallel sentences. The analogy can be fine mid-paragraph. The matched-pair drumroll at the end is the slop. The fix: if the explanation already made the point, stop. If the image earns its place, work it into the prose instead of the parallel-pair flourish.

## Imperative-Consequence Couplets

A short command paired with a vague result, in a two-beat cadence that sounds like a systems insight but names no mechanism you can act on. Often it just restates the sentence before it with less information.

- "Touch one, the other shifts."
- "Pull one thread and it all unravels."
- "Move one piece, the whole board changes."
- "Shift one, the rest follow."
- "Push here, it gives there."
- "Change one thing and everything changes."

Why it's a tell: it gestures at cause and effect without saying what causes what. "Touch one, the other shifts" could describe any two connected things in any system, which is the generic test failing.

The fix: name the actual mechanism. "Touch one, the other shifts" becomes "sell one unit and stock drops by one." If you can't say what specifically moves and by how much, cut the line; the dependency wasn't worth stating.

## Synonym Cycling

Using two or three near-synonyms in sequence to pad a sentence.

- "transform, shift, and evolve"
- "authentic, genuine, and real"
- "clarity, insight, and understanding"

The fix: pick the one that's most precise. Delete the other two.

## Summary-as-Conclusion

Restating the essay's points as a bulleted or numbered list of "We can..." or "You can..." statements at the end. It reads like a recap slide at the end of a corporate presentation. Essays aren't presentations. They should land, not summarize.

The fix: end with something specific. A single concrete image, a question that actually stings, or the one sentence you'd want someone to remember. Not a table of contents for the piece they just read.

## Stealth Adverbs

Adverbs that add mood but no meaning. The sentence already says the thing; the adverb only sets a tone. Test: delete the adverb. If the sentence means the same thing, it was decoration.

Banned when they're load-bearing on tone alone:

- quietly
- gently
- softly
- simply
- just
- slowly
- casually
- merely

- "so I cannot quietly talk myself out of it" becomes "so I cannot talk myself out of it"
- "it simply works" becomes "it works"

Keep them only when they carry real information. "She closed the door quietly" states a physical fact and stays.

## Fragments Posing as Sentences

Noun phrases dressed up as standalone sentences for rhythm or emphasis. Every sentence needs a subject and a verb. Test: find the verb. If there isn't one, fold the fragment into a real sentence.

- "Forty-five minutes before work, four mornings a week." becomes "I put in forty-five minutes before work, four mornings a week."

Note: this targets AI-generated copy. A writer's own essay voice may use fragments deliberately. Respect those; only kill the fragments Claude generates.

## Mismatched Collocations

Word pairs that sound fluent but don't combine into a real meaning, usually an abstract noun handed a verb it can't take. Test: picture the action literally. If the noun can't perform it, rewrite.

- "fluency actually arrives" (fluency doesn't arrive anywhere)
- "planned anything complex for a living" (nobody plans "anything complex" for a living)

The fix: cut the clause or swap the abstraction for the concrete thing. "compounds over months, which is how fluency arrives" just stops at "compounds over months."

## The Generic Test

After writing anything, apply this test:

1. Replace your product/company/client name with a competitor's name
2. Does the copy still make sense?
3. If yes, it's too generic. Rewrite until swapping names would break it.

## Encyclopedic Puffery Vocabulary

Words AI reaches for to sound authoritative and weighty. They inflate importance without adding a fact. Most can be deleted or swapped for the concrete thing.

- "delve" / "delve into"
- "stands as a testament to"
- "a testament to"
- "rich tapestry" / "rich cultural heritage"
- "underscores" (as in "underscores its significance")
- "showcasing" / "showcases"
- "boasts" (as in "the city boasts")
- "nestled" (as in "nestled in the hills")
- "in the realm of"
- "plays a vital role" / "plays a significant role" / "plays a pivotal role"
- "leaves a lasting impact" / "leaves a lasting impression"
- "a beacon of"
- "treasure trove"
- "vibrant" (as a default scene-setter)
- "ever-evolving" / "ever-changing"
- "watershed moment"
- "garnered" (as in "garnered attention")
- "stands out as"
- "continues to captivate"

The fix: name the actual thing. "The museum boasts a vast collection" becomes "the museum holds 4,000 works." "Plays a vital role in the community" becomes "runs the only food bank in the county." If you can't name the concrete fact, the sentence is decoration.

## Vague Attribution

Opinion or claims attributed to an unnamed crowd of authorities. It manufactures consensus and dodges the burden of a real source. High-signal AI tell.

- "some critics argue"
- "many experts believe"
- "observers note"
- "industry reports suggest"
- "studies suggest" (with no study named)
- "it is widely regarded as"
- "some have argued"
- "critics and supporters alike"
- "it is often said that"

The fix: name the source or cut the claim. "Some experts believe remote work boosts output" becomes "A 2023 Stanford study found remote workers were 13% more productive," or it gets deleted. If you can't attribute it, don't smuggle it in behind a vague crowd.

## Editorializing Asides

Phrases that flag a point as important instead of just making it important. They are filler that announces filler.

- "it's important to note that"
- "it's worth noting that"
- "it is important to remember"
- "no discussion of X would be complete without"
- "it should be noted that"
- "interestingly,"
- "notably," (when it adds nothing)

The fix: delete the aside and state the point directly. If the point is worth making, its content shows that, not a label telling the reader to pay attention.

## AI Meta-Leakage

Artifacts of the chat interface that leak into finished copy. Instant giveaways that nobody edited the output.

- "As an AI language model,"
- "I hope this helps!"
- "Certainly! Here's"
- "Sure! Here is"
- "Is there anything else I can help with?"
- "As of my last knowledge update,"
- "as of my knowledge cutoff"
- "I cannot provide" / "I'm unable to" (in delivered copy)
- "Feel free to let me know"

The fix: these never belong in a deliverable. Strip every line that addresses the reader as a chatbot would. The copy should read as if a person wrote it for a specific purpose, with no trace of the request that produced it.

## Heading Tells

How AI formats section headings, distinct from the words inside them.

- Title Case On Every Main Word ("Early Life And Career")
- The "A and B" conjunction heading ("Awards and Recognition", "Content and Features", "Challenges and Opportunities", "Background and History")

The fix: use sentence case unless a house style requires otherwise ("Early life and career"). Split or rename padded "A and B" headings into something that names the actual content.

## Mechanical Boldface

Bolding applied to every instance of a chosen term, or to a phrase in nearly every bullet, the way a sales deck or fan wiki does. It signals emphasis without earning it and flattens real emphasis when it matters.

- Bolding the lead phrase of every item in a list
- Bolding every occurrence of a product or person's name
- Bolding whole sentences for "impact"

The fix: bold sparingly, and only where one specific word genuinely needs to stand out. If everything is bold, nothing is. Default to no bold and add it back only when a reader would miss the point without it.

## Typographic Quote Marks

Curly/smart quotation marks and apostrophes that some models default to. Minor on its own, but a consistent tell when combined with others.

- Smart quotes and curly apostrophes in place of straight ones

The fix: match whatever the surrounding document uses. In plain-text and code contexts use straight quotes. This is low priority next to the items above.

## Fabricated Citations

AI invents sources that look real and aren't. This extends Law #7. When citing anything:

- Do not generate a citation, DOI, ISBN, URL, author, or publication date you have not verified exists.
- A plausible-looking reference is not a real one. Fabricated citations are worse than no citation.

The fix: cite only what you can confirm. If a claim needs a source you don't have, say so and leave it unsourced rather than inventing one.

## Personal Banned Words

Every writer has a few words they never want in their own copy. Those live in the reader's personal rules file in their workspace (`00_Shelf/writing-rules.md` in a Claude OS), added when they ask Claude to update their writing rules. This shipped list stays fixed; the personal file is the one that grows. Keep each entry concrete: the word, the abstract use to kill, and the literal use to keep.

Example entry:

- "land / lands / landing" used as a verb for where information or work ends up ("each clip lands in the right topic", "the message landed"). Literal physical uses are fine ("the plane lands"). The fix: name the concrete action or place, for example "each clip files itself under the right topic."
