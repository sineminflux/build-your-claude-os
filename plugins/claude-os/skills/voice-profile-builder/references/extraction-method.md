# Extraction Method

The analytical process for turning writing samples into a voice profile. Every finding maps back to a specific line in the samples. No archetypes, no filling gaps from templates, no vibe-based description. If the pattern isn't in the writing, it doesn't go in the file.

Work the layers in order. Each builds on the last.

---

## Layer 1: Mechanics, measured

Adjectives ("punchy," "conversational") are weak instructions. Numbers are strong ones. Measure, from the samples:

- **Sentence length:** median and mean words per sentence. The median is the baseline rhythm.
- **Variance:** the share of very short sentences (5 words or fewer) and long ones (25+ words). A real voice varies. If everything is 12 to 18 words, that itself is a machine tell to avoid.
- **The long tail:** note whether they actually write long, comma-driven building sentences. Most people do more than they think. Don't let the file tell them to write shorter than they do.
- **Contraction density,** and whether it changes by register (often higher in personal or reflective writing, lower when teaching or arguing a framework).
- **Paragraph shape:** the share of one-sentence paragraphs.
- **Punctuation fingerprint:** counts of ellipses, parentheses, em dashes, semicolons, exclamation marks, rhetorical questions. These are highly individual.

An AI can compute all of this from pasted samples. Ask it to. Numbers keep the profile honest and stop it from drifting into generic description.

---

## Layer 2: Punctuation and emphasis habits

The individual marks they lean on and the ones they never touch. Ellipses for hesitation or a reveal, parentheticals for the aside or the wink, colons to set up a line, caps or period-spacing for emphasis, italics for a mantra or a title, bold for a reframe. Note the bans too (no em dashes, no semicolons, or whatever is true), because absence is as defining as presence.

---

## Layer 3: Structure and signature moves

The observable moves that make the voice recognizable.

- **Openers:** how they start. A scene, a date, a claim, a question, a tally, a stacked hook, a confession. And the openers they never use.
- **The spine:** do they build a piece around one image or named concept? Note it.
- **The metaphor well:** when they reach for an analogy, what domain does it come from (science, sport, cooking, nature, finance)? Naming the well keeps future drafts from reaching into the wrong one.
- **Development:** how they move between ideas, whether they number stages, quote a source then translate it, bold the reframe.
- **Closers:** how they end. A question handed to the reader, a callback, a concrete image, a call to action. And what they never do (summary, zoom-out, TED-talk wrap).

Pull a real example for each. The move isn't real unless you can point to it.

---

## Layer 4: The never-list (tailored, not generic)

What breaks this voice. Do NOT paste a generic 40-word list of AI cliches. A bloated, generic never-list gets followed worse. Keep only the traps that are actually this person's risk: the tics you saw creeping into their own writing, the registers they'd never use (corporate, hustle-bro, guru), and their single biggest dealbreaker, marked loud. A shared anti-slop rule set covers the general cliches. This list is the personal layer.

---

## Layer 5: The words they actually use

The other side of the never-list. Their signature phrases and openers (with rough counts, so an AI knows which are frequent), their filler words (to use naturally and trim when they pile up), and the words only they reach for. Include a caution: recognizing that someone uses a device (a mantra, a fragment, a triad) is not permission for an AI to manufacture fresh instances of it. Match the spirit, don't clone the trick.

---

## Layer 6: Register, how the voice flexes by platform

The voice is one person everywhere. What changes is length, formatting, and heat. Build a small table across the platforms they actually use (email, LinkedIn, Instagram, newsletter, DMs), with length and shape, formatting habits, tone, and how each piece tends to end. Mark any row you're inferring rather than measuring, and say which registers need more samples.

---

## Layer 7: Before-and-after calibration pairs

The single most useful thing for an AI to imitate. Three to five pairs: a generic, could-be-anyone version, then the same idea in the real voice. Make each "after" a real line the person wrote wherever possible, so the contrast is their actual voice doing the correcting, not an invention. Add a one-line note on which patterns are at work.

---

## Sample size and inconsistency

Quality and variety beat volume. Note the limits honestly in the output ("built from 9 long-form pieces, so the email register is thin"). If you find variation, first check whether it's contextual (different platform, different audience), in which case it's range, not inconsistency, and belongs in the register table. If it's evolution over time, weight recent samples higher and note the shift. If it's genuinely inconsistent, ask which direction feels more like them, with concrete examples from both.
