# Calibration and Interview

Two things that turn a decent voice file into an accurate one: a short interview that fills what the samples can't show, and a calibration loop that proves the voice before you trust it.

---

## The interview (fill the gaps, don't replace the samples)

Extract the voice from the writing first. Use the interview only for what the samples leave ambiguous. Keep every question closed and evidence-based. Never ask an open question about something the writing already answers.

Good questions to ask, only where the samples are unclear:

- "I measured your median sentence at [X] words with a long tail of [Y]% at 25+ words. Keep that tail, or do you want tighter?"
- "You open [N] of [M] pieces with a concrete scene. Is that the opener that feels most like you, or is it habit you'd drop?"
- "Your never-list from your own writing looks like [3 items]. Which is the single tell that instantly reads as not-you?"
- "You use [device] a lot. Is that deliberate, or a filler you'd rather I trim?"
- "In emails you get to the ask in the first two lines, but essays wander for three paragraphs. Is that the split you want, or should I flag one?"

Batch the high-confidence findings ("I locked these five in from your writing, tell me if any is off") and slow to single questions only where you're genuinely unsure. Thorough does not mean thirty one-at-a-time prompts.

Show evidence whenever you infer: a number, a quoted line, or a count. Confidence comes from proof, not assertion.

---

## The calibration loop (this is what makes it accurate)

Before you call the profile done, test it against the person's own gut.

1. Pick the two contexts that matter most to them (for example an email and a post, or their top two platforms). Write each one fully in the new voice, applying everything: mechanics, signature moves, the never-list, the register shift.
2. Show both. Ask a closed question for each: (a) nailed it, (b) close, one thing off, (c) not me. And: tell me exactly what's off, a word, a rhythm, a vibe.
3. Take every correction literally and turn it into a rule in the file. This overrides earlier guesses. If they say "that's not me," don't defend the draft, find the rule that failed and fix it.
4. Re-draft anything that got a (b) or (c) and check again. Loop until both read as "that's me." Don't proceed on a "close."
5. If they struggle to name what's wrong, write the same piece two ways and ask which is more them. Keep the winner's traits.

Whatever you learn here is the truest signal you'll get. Weight it above every earlier inference.

A real example of the loop working: a first draft opened with "Today I'm changing what this is about." The writer flagged it as too vague, said she'd never open that way. That became a rule (no vague change-announcement openers, drop into the concrete moment instead) and every later draft honored it. One correction, one permanent rule.

---

## Keep it a living profile

- **Learn from edits.** Put a line high in the principles file: if the writer corrects a draft, treat that edit as a correction to the file, fix the rule, and follow it from then on.
- **Version it.** Add a dated changelog. Increment on refinements, bump a major version when the voice has clearly evolved.
- **Refresh.** Re-run the extraction on new writing every couple of months, weighting recent and published work highest. Voices move, especially for someone whose lane is still forming.
- **Close the gaps.** Whichever registers were inferred rather than measured (often email and DMs) get filled first when samples become available.

---

## Handling a voice that's still forming

If the samples pull in two directions, don't average them into mush. Decide, with the writer, which voice they want to amplify. The goal is not to document everything they've ever written. It's to capture the voice they want more of.
